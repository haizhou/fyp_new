# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## 1.1 Task and motivation

Every year the United Kingdom publishes hundreds of thousands of contract-award notices describing how public money is spent: which authority bought what, from which supplier, for how much, and under which procurement category. This dissertation concerns natural-language question answering over a knowledge graph built from 215,221 such contract-award records [PENDING: promote to results_master_table.md from kg_enrichment_plan.md]. A journalist, auditor, or council officer should be able to ask "Which supplier received the most cleaning contracts from London boroughs in 2022?" and receive either an answer whose every step is traceable to specific records in the graph, or an explicit refusal to answer when the question is ambiguous, unsupported by the schema, or matched by no records at all.

The domain makes the second half of that requirement as important as the first. Questions about public expenditure carry an accountability weight that most question-answering settings do not: a fluent but fabricated total, or a confidently named supplier who never held the contract, is not merely unhelpful — it is actively worse than no answer, because it can propagate into reporting and decision-making with the borrowed authority of the underlying official data. The system studied here, CICADA, is therefore designed around a single non-negotiable constraint: an answer is only ever produced by deterministic execution against the knowledge graph, and where the system cannot support an answer it must abstain.

Large language models are the obvious candidate technology for the language-understanding half of this task, and the largest cloud-hosted models can indeed perform it to a useful standard. But deploying a cloud model against public-sector data raises three coupled objections. It is costly, because every query incurs marginal API expenditure that scales with usage rather than amortising. It is uncontrollable, because the provider may change, deprecate, or nondeterministically vary the model underneath a fixed application — a variability we measure directly in this work. And it is non-private, because the queries themselves, and any internal data joined against them, must leave the organisation's infrastructure. A local system built from small open-weight models would resolve all three objections at once — if it could be made competent enough.

## 1.2 The trust dilemma, and why retrieval augmentation is not enough

The task sits on the horns of a trust dilemma. Language models can read the question but answer unverifiably: nothing in a free-form generated response distinguishes a genuine aggregation over the data from a plausible confabulation. Query executors answer verifiably but cannot read: a database engine will faithfully compute whatever it is asked, but converting an ambiguous English question into the right query is precisely the hard part. Any credible architecture must couple the two, and the coupling point is where trust is either established or quietly lost.

The most common contemporary coupling — retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), in which relevant records are retrieved into the model's context window and the model composes an answer over them — fails this task structurally rather than incidentally. Procurement questions are dominated by exhaustive computation: counts, sums, rankings, and multi-hop joins whose correct answer depends on *all* matching records, not a representative sample. No top-k retrieval window can contain "every contract awarded by every London borough in 2022", and a language model asked to aggregate over a truncated window will produce an answer that is wrong in a way no reader can detect. This is borne out empirically: on our 260-question development set under the v1 pipeline, a naive RAG baseline achieved 31.5% (82/260) and a strengthened RAG baseline 28.8% (75/260), while under the same v1 pipeline and on the same development set a completely untrained 8B model, given only the executable-plan scaffolding described in Chapter 5, reached 61.5% (160/260). (Under the revised v2.2 pipeline this zero-shot scaffolding floor rises to 70.4% on the same development set.) The lesson is that the scaffolding — forcing the model to emit a checkable, executable plan rather than a free-form answer — is worth more than retrieval, before any training has taken place.

Scaffolding alone, however, leaves a gap of ten to twenty accuracy points against what the task demands, and closing that gap is where the second dilemma appears. The classical route to a competent small model is distillation from a large one: the cloud teacher labels training data, and the student imitates. But classical distillation presupposes a *trusted* teacher, and on this task the teacher is demonstrably not trustworthy — as Chapter 6 shows, the cloud teacher pipeline itself answers only around seven in ten held-out questions correctly. Imitating an untrusted teacher transfers its errors along with its competence. The central move of this dissertation is to show that teacher trust can be substituted by something the task itself provides: *partial verifiability*. A large and precisely characterisable region of the failure space — whether a plan compiles, whether its entities ground in the graph, whether it executes, whether its structure matches its stated intent, and, at training time only, whether its answer agrees with an independent oracle — is mechanically checkable. Supervision can be drawn exclusively from that region, discarding everything the checks cannot vouch for, regardless of which model produced it.

## 1.3 The one-sentence thesis

The dissertation defends a single sentence:

> **Supervision flows only from the verifiable region, yet the learned competence extends beyond it.**

The first clause is a statement of construction. Every training example used anywhere in this work has passed a deterministic compile–ground–execute–verify chain, and — during training-data harvest only — an agreement check against an independently computed oracle answer. The oracle *filters* candidate examples; it never *authors* them: no gold plan or gold answer is ever copied into the training data. Whatever cannot be projected onto structure and execution — the purely semantic question of whether a plan means what the question means — receives no supervision at all, because no mechanical check can certify it.

The second clause is the empirical surprise, and the mechanism behind the headline results. If the student merely memorised the verifiable region, its competence should stop at the projection line: it should perform well where checks reach and collapse where they do not. It does not. On a development-set slice analysis contrasting an i.i.d. slice (n=103) with a *hard-composite* slice (n=136) — questions combining hard operators, second-level paraphrase, and abstention cases, a difficulty composite present in both training and test with disjoint plans and novel surfaces — the cloud teacher's accuracy decays by between 6 and 12 points across three replicates (−10.3, −6.1, −12.5), while the fully-local student shows no measurable decay (+0.8 points, n=136). The student trained only on verifier-passed data generalises *past* the region the verifier can see, where the far larger teacher degrades. This is the mechanism answer to the question "why is partial verifiability enough": the capability learned from verifiable supervision is wider than the coverage of the signal that taught it.

## 1.4 Approach overview: the three-layer structure

The system design that operationalises this thesis has three layers, treated fully in Chapter 5 and summarised here.

**The hard-verification layer.** Every property along the semantic chain from question to answer that *can* be projected onto structure or execution *is* projected, and pinned by a deterministic check: the executor's behaviour is pinned to the plan graph by compilation and execution; the plan graph is pinned to the intermediate briefing by structural comparison in code; slots, entities, literals, numbers, roles, and cue words are cross-checked between briefing and question. It must be said precisely: these checks do not verify everything, and this dissertation never claims that they do. Everything hard-verifiable is hard-verified; the residue is squeezed to a minimum; and the checks shrink the blind region layer by layer — they never eliminate it.

**The abstention layer.** The unprojectable residue — chiefly, whether the plan's meaning matches the question's meaning — constitutes a blind region, and the system's answer to the blind region is calibrated refusal. Abstention is a first-class output, and the benchmark contains three families of abstention traps (ambiguous questions, schema-unsupported questions, empty-result questions) that serve as the completeness test of this layer rather than as decoration.

**The learning layer.** Signal flowing out of the verifiable region drives distillation and bootstrapping: teacher outputs are filtered rather than trusted, student outputs are self-harvested through the same filter, and the filtered pool trains both stages of the stack. The learned competence then covers part of the blind region — which is the one-sentence thesis restated as an engineering loop.

## 1.5 Contributions

The dissertation makes four contributions, referred to throughout as the four pillars.

**Pillar 1 — the bootstrap closed loop (Chapter 6).** Three instantiations of learning from the verifiable region: filtering an untrusted cloud teacher's outputs into clean supervision; a self-harvest round in which the student, generating and filtering its own training data, overtakes the teacher that taught it; and a denoising distillation of the first pipeline stage (understanding/briefing) that lets the entire stack run locally. Together these bootstrap both stages of a neuro-symbolic system past both of its cloud teachers, over a 12,828-question benchmark (9,267 train / 556 dev-tune / 671 dev-select / 49 dev-smoke / 2,285 final test).

**Pillar 2 — gain decomposition (Chapters 5–6).** A separation of the hard-verification layer's contribution from the learning layer's: the zero-shot scaffolding floor measures what deterministic checking and repair buy before any training, and the training ladder above it measures what verifier-filtered learning adds. The development-set ladder establishes that the decomposition exists; all confirmatory claims are carried by the final test set.

**Pillar 3 — DPO near-miss toxicity (Chapter 7).** A mechanism-explained negative result: using verifier-passing-but-wrong outputs as preference-learning negatives, natural as it seems, can suppress the very competencies the positives teach, and the contrast between subtractive preference suppression and additive distillation explains when each is safe.

**Pillar 4 — benchmark methodology (Chapter 4).** The abstention layer's test apparatus and the credibility foundation under every number in this dissertation: dual-implementation oracles whose agreement is measured rather than assumed, plan-level split isolation so that no test plan is seen in training, and a strict development/confirmatory discipline in which model selection and pipeline fixes are derived exclusively from development data.

## 1.6 Results preview

The confirmatory evaluation is a five-system pairing on the held-out final test set (n=2,285, v4.1 benchmark, evaluation protocol with repair budget 1), covering the cloud teacher, two hybrid students (cloud understanding stage, local planning stage), and two fully-local students (both stages local 8B LoRA adapters), on two independent base models.

| System | Final-test accuracy |
|---|---|
| Teacher (cloud, 1 replicate) | 69.76% (1594/2285) |
| Llama-3.1-8B hybrid | 75.97% |
| Qwen3-8B hybrid | 78.03% |
| Llama-3.1-8B fully local | 83.33% |
| Qwen3-8B fully local | **85.65% (1957/2285)** |

Every adjacent and non-adjacent pairing in this scoreboard is significant at McNemar p < 1e-14. The headline: the fully-local Qwen3-8B stack exceeds its own cloud teacher by **+15.89 points** (95% CI [+14.07, +17.70], discordant pairs +406/−43, p < 1e-15), and the result replicates on a second base model, with the fully-local Llama-3.1-8B stack at 83.33% (+13.57 points over the teacher, CI [+11.81, +15.32]). Because the teacher was run as a single replicate on the final test set, the robustness of the student–teacher gap to provider nondeterminism is established separately on the development set, where the student beats each of three independent teacher replicates (discordant pairs +35/−5, +34/−4, +30/−5; p ≤ 2e-5). The fully-local stack achieves this at zero marginal API cost, on a single local GPU, with no data leaving the machine.

## 1.7 Dissertation structure

Chapter 2 surveys the four literatures the work sits between and draws the dividing line against its nearest neighbours on filter strength and blind-region handling. Chapter 3 describes the procurement data and the construction of the knowledge graph. Chapter 4 presents the benchmark and its integrity apparatus (Pillar 4). Chapter 5 presents the three-layer system and the deterministic check chain, and measures the scaffolding floor (Pillar 2, verification half). Chapter 6 presents the bootstrap experiments — teacher filtering, self-harvest, and denoising distillation — culminating in the five-system scoreboard above (Pillars 1 and 2, learning half). Chapter 7 analyses the DPO near-miss pathology and the mechanism evidence for the one-sentence thesis (Pillar 3). Chapter 8 concludes with limitations, including the LLM-generated character of the benchmark surfaces and the boundaries of the strict-holdout evidence, and with directions for moving more of the blind region across the projection line.
